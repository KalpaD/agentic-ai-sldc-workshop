Part 1 of 3

Context Engineering

A grand name for a simple idea: deciding what your agent knows before it answers. This is the difference between an agent that feels clever and one that feels useless.
What context is Why it runs out Weak vs strong Four techniques
Say plainly that "context engineering" sounds far more technical than it is. By the end of this part they should be able to explain it to a colleague in one sentence.
Plain definition

Context is everything your agent can see right now

Not everything it has ever read — everything in front of it at the moment you press Enter. It answers using that, and nothing else.

What people assume

"It knows my project. I told it about the bakery an hour ago."

So they write short prompts and wonder why the answers drift.

What is actually true

It knows exactly what is in front of it: your message, your AGENTS.md, the files it has opened, and the conversation so far.

Anything outside that does not exist to it.

The mental model to break is the idea that the agent "remembers" like a person. It does not - it re-reads. Everything today follows from that one correction.
Look inside

Four things fill the context

You control all four. That is the whole of context engineering.
Walk the four boxes slowly. Point out that they already control number two - they wrote an AGENTS.md yesterday. The rest of this part is about the other three.
The catch

The window has a fixed size — measured in tokens

Remember tokens from Day 1? The context window holds a fixed number of them. Fill it up, and the oldest parts get pushed out to make room — which is exactly when an agent starts "forgetting" what you agreed earlier.

Early in a chat

Plenty of room. It remembers everything you've said. Answers feel sharp.

An hour later

The window is full of old messages. Your important rule from earlier has been squeezed out.

What you notice

It contradicts itself, forgets the file structure, or re-does work. Nothing is broken — the window is simply full.

This is the slide that explains an experience they will definitely have this week. Naming it now means that when it happens on Day 4 they diagnose it instead of assuming they broke something.
The same request, twice

Weak context vs strong context

Identical prompt. The only difference is what the agent could see when it answered.

Weak — fresh session, nothing else

"add a contact section"

What comes back: a contact form with name, email, message. Styled in a way that matches nothing. Saved wherever it felt like.

It guessed, because it had to.
Strong — AGENTS.md + the page open

"add a contact section"

What comes back: a section matching the page's fonts and colours, with the bakery's real phone number, added to the existing file.

Same six words. Better answer.
This is the demo slide. If you can, run both live rather than just describing them - a fresh session versus the real project makes the point far harder than any slide can. Six identical words, two very different results.
Technique 1 of 4

Write the rules down once — AGENTS.md

You did this yesterday. It is the single highest-value thing you can put in context, because it is re-read before every task and never gets squeezed out.

AGENTS.md
# Sunrise Bakery — website ## Stack - One page: index.html - Plain CSS in style.css - No frameworks, no JavaScript ## Style - Warm, friendly, simple - Big readable text ## Rules - Keep all files in this folder - Ask before adding anything new
  1. 1
    Stack stops the guessingWithout this it may reach for a framework you've never heard of.
  2. 2
    Style is context too"Warm and friendly" genuinely changes the words and colours it picks.
  3. 3
    Rules survive the whole sessionUnlike something you said forty messages ago.
The style section is the part worth dwelling on - most people think AGENTS.md is only for technical facts. Telling it the tone you want is context engineering just as much as naming the stack.
Technique 2 of 4

Point at the file you mean

If your request is about something that already exists, say which file. It costs you four words and removes all the guessing.

Vague

"make the heading bigger"

Which heading? In which file? It may open three files looking, or change the wrong one.

Pointed

"in index.html, make the main heading at the top bigger"

It opens one file, changes one thing, and you can check it in seconds.

Small technique, big effect, and easy to teach students. Naming the file also keeps the context window tidy because the agent stops opening files speculatively.
Technique 3 of 4

Start a fresh session when you change job

A long conversation carries everything that came before it. When you move to a new task, all that old detail is no longer help — it is clutter competing for space.

Keep the same session

While you're still working on the same thing — building the page, then fixing the page, then styling the page. The history is genuinely useful.

Start a new one

When you switch to something unrelated — from the bakery page to marking a quiz. Type /new and begin clean.

Think of it as

Wiping the whiteboard between two lessons. Not because the last lesson was wrong — because leaving it up confuses the next one.

/new is the practical takeaway here. Most beginners never start a new session and then wonder why quality degrades over an afternoon. Tell them it is free and nothing is lost from disk.
Technique 4 of 4

Give it enough — not everything

The instinct once you understand context is to pour in as much as possible. That backfires: the important detail gets buried among the unimportant.

Too much

Pasting a whole textbook chapter, every file in the folder, and three previous lesson plans — to ask for one paragraph.

The one line that mattered gets lost.
Enough

The relevant file, the rule that applies, and a clear request.

Nothing competing for its attention.
Worth saying out loud because it is counter-intuitive: more context is not better context. Relevance beats volume every time.
Keep this

When an answer disappoints, check these four

Ask yourselfThe fix
Did it know the rules of this project?Put them in AGENTS.md — once, permanently.
Did it know which file I meant?Name the file in your request.
Have we been talking for a long time?Start fresh with /new.
Did I bury the important part?Cut the rest. Give it what matters and nothing else.

Nine times out of ten a disappointing answer is a context problem, not a model problem.

This table is in their student guide. It is the most practically useful page of the day - encourage them to try the four questions before ever blaming the tool.
Part 2 of 3

Many Hands

Once you understand context, sub-agents make sense — because the real reason to split a job is to give each part its own clean context.
Why split a job The built-in helpers Each gets its own context Keeping them aligned
This part is short and conceptual - about ten minutes. Resist expanding it; the understanding lands properly during the build.
Reminder from Day 1

You already have helpers — you don't build them

OpenCode ships with these. You call one by typing @ and its name, exactly like tagging a colleague in a message.

@explore

Searches your files and reports back. Read-only — it cannot change anything.

@scout

Looks things up outside your project. Also read-only.

@general

Takes on a whole piece of work on its own. Full access.

Why read-only matters

You can hand @explore a job with no risk at all. It can tell you what's wrong with your page, but it cannot touch it. We'll use exactly that later as our reviewer.

The read-only point is worth landing because it is genuinely reassuring - a reviewer that physically cannot break your work is an easy idea to trust, and it is how we use it in the build.
The real reason to split

Each helper gets its own clean context

This is why sub-agents produce better work — not because they are cleverer, but because each one only sees what its own job needs.

Think of it as

Group work in your classroom. You don't give every group the whole project brief — you give each the part they need, then bring the pieces together yourself.

Connect this straight back to Part 1: a helper with a small clean context does better work than one agent juggling everything. That is the same lesson, applied to teams.
The obvious risk

If each sees something different, they drift apart

Three helpers working separately can easily produce three styles that don't match. There is one thing that keeps them aligned — and you already have it.

Without shared rules

The words are formal, the layout is playful, and the reviewer judges it against something else entirely.

Three good pieces that don't fit.
With AGENTS.md

Every helper reads the same file before starting. Same stack, same tone, same rules.

One page that looks deliberate.
This is why we write AGENTS.md first in the build, before any helper is called. Say that now so the order of the next section makes sense.

First helper — write the words

Notice we ask for only the words. No HTML yet. One job, one helper, one clean context.

Do now Tag a helper with @

Two things to point out. First, invented details are the agent filling gaps in a thin brief - a perfect live example of why context matters. Second, separating words from layout is exactly how a real design team works.

Second helper — build the page around those words

This helper's context is different: it needs the words and the rules, but not the conversation about writing them.

Do now Point it at the file the last helper made

You named the file — technique 2 from this morning — so this helper never had to guess where the words were.

Do now · Step 5 of 6

Third helper — a reviewer that cannot break anything

Here is where read-only earns its keep. @explore can read every file and tell you what's wrong, but it physically cannot change your page.

Do now Ask for a review, not a fix
> @explore read index.html, style.css and AGENTS.md, then tell me anything that breaks the rules in AGENTS.md or looks wrong on a phone. Just report — do not change anything.
You should see A short list of problems. Common ones: a framework crept in, text too small on a phone, or a section from content.md that never made it onto the page.
If a review comes back with nothing at all, that is usually a sign it did not really look - ask it to check against each rule in AGENTS.md one at a time. A reviewer that never finds anything is not a useful reviewer.
Do now · Step 6 of 6

Hand the findings back to be fixed

The reviewer found the problems. Now the main agent fixes them — which is exactly how a real team works.

Do now Two steps: fix, then look again
> fix everything on that list, then tell me what you changed then refresh the page in your browser — > @explore check it again
Watch for The second review should be shorter than the first. That gap is the whole value of having a reviewer.
✋ Checkpoint

A working page, reviewed and corrected, built by three helpers. Everyone should be here before we go on.

Point out the loop from Day 1 running at full size: describe, act, verify, repeat - only now the verify step is being done by another agent rather than by them.
Now the fun part

Make it yours

Ten minutes to change whatever you like. Try one thing at a time and refresh the browser after each — that habit is what makes problems easy to find.

Make it real

"Change the opening hours to 6am–7pm and the phone number to my school's."

Change the feel

"Make the colours cooler and more modern." Then ask for warm again and compare.

Add something

"Add a section listing three cakes with prices." Watch it obey AGENTS.md while doing it.

Remember

Don't start over. Say what's wrong and let it fix that one thing — the rule from Day 1 that you'll rely on completely tomorrow.

Free exploration matters after a long lockstep stretch. Circulate rather than presenting. Ask a few people to show what they changed at the end of the ten minutes.

Being honest

When not to bother with helpers

Splitting a job has a cost — you have to brief each helper and join the pieces up. For small work that cost is more than the benefit.

Don't split

"Fix this typo."
"Make the heading bigger."
"Add my phone number."

One agent, one message. Done.
Do split

Work with clearly different jobs inside it — writing versus building versus checking. Or when you want a reviewer who cannot change your work.

Worth the briefing.
Important for credibility. If you present multi-agent as always better they will over-use it, get slower results, and quietly conclude the whole idea was oversold.
