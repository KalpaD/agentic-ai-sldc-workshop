Part 1 of 4

AI & Generative AI

Before we talk about agents, let's be precise about what the thing underneath them actually is — and what it isn't.
What AI means What "generative" adds How it really works Where you see it
Keep this section moving. It is grounding, not the main event — but the "how it really works" slide matters because every limitation later comes from it.
Definition

Ordinary software follows rules. AI learns patterns.

This is the whole difference, and it explains everything that follows.

Ordinary software

A person writes every rule in advance:

IF subject contains "lottery"
THEN mark as spam

Only handles what was predicted.
AI software

Nobody writes the rules. It is shown thousands of examples of spam and not-spam, and works out the pattern itself.

Handles things nobody predicted.
Use the spam example because everyone has an inbox. The point to land: with AI, no human wrote the rule that caught the new kind of spam — the machine inferred it from examples.
The word "generative"

Older AI decides. Generative AI creates.

The difference is the shape of what comes out. One returns a verdict; the other returns something new that did not exist before.

Decides — pick from options

"Is this email spam?"
→ yes / no

"What language is this?"
→ Sinhala

Output: a choice.
Creates — makes something new

"Write a reply to this parent."
→ a full paragraph

"Build me a webpage."
→ working code

Output: a new thing.
Everything this week lives in the right-hand column. Code is just another thing generative AI can create — that is the bridge into coding agents.
Under the bonnet

It is predicting the next piece, over and over

A generative AI is not looking up answers in a database. It has read an enormous amount of text and learned which words tend to follow which. Ask it something, and it predicts the most likely next word — then the next, then the next.

"The capital of Sri Lanka is Sri Jayawardenepura Kotte" ← each word predicted from everything before it
Why this matters

This is why it can write something brand new — and also why it sometimes states a wrong thing with total confidence. It is producing what sounds most likely, not looking up a verified fact. Remember this when we discuss its limits.

This is the single most useful slide for demystifying AI. Once teachers understand "predicting the next likely word", both the magic and the mistakes make sense. They can teach this exact explanation to students. Then say: that "piece" has a proper name — and go to the next slide.
The unit it actually works in

Those pieces have a name — tokens

An AI model doesn't see letters, and it doesn't see whole sentences. It breaks text into chunks called tokens. A common word is usually one token; a longer or unusual word gets split into several.

A simple sentence — every common word is one token
Thecatsatonthemat. 7 tokens
A longer word gets broken into pieces
Photosynthesisisfascinating. 6 tokens
Rule of thumb

Roughly 4 characters ≈ 1 token, so about 750 words ≈ 1,000 tokens. The exact split differs between AI models — you never need to count them yourself, but it helps to know the unit exists.

Read the first strip aloud chip by chip so they see the sentence being chopped up. The second strip is the surprise — "Photosynthesis" is one word to us but several tokens to the machine. Stress that they never have to count tokens by hand; this is about recognising the word when they meet it.
How text gets in and out

Tokens in, tokens out — nothing else

Your question is turned into tokens before the model ever sees it. The model then produces its answer one token at a time, and those tokens are turned back into words on your screen.

Why you'll care on Day 2

Everything is measured in tokens — including the free allowance we all use this week. When someone's agent pauses and says "rate limit", it means they've sent too many tokens too quickly. Now you know exactly what that message means.

The practical payoff is the last box: tokens are the unit the free tier counts, so this slide is what makes the Day 2 and Day 4 rate-limit messages make sense instead of feeling like a failure. Also point out that answers streaming in word by word is literally the model emitting one token at a time.
Where you already see it

The same technology, three kinds of output

Lesson plans, exam questions, letters to parents, report comments, translations.

Images

Illustrations for worksheets, diagrams, slide backgrounds, poster art.

Code

Working software — websites, apps, databases. This is the one we spend the rest of the week on.

Code turns out to be the thing generative AI is unusually good at, because code has strict, checkable rules — the machine can run it and immediately see whether it worked.

That last line is important and often surprising: code is easier for AI than essays, because correctness is testable. It sets up why agents can self-correct.

The problem with chat AI

ChatGPT gives you homework, not a finished job

Imagine you ask ChatGPT to build a page for your school's sports day. Here is what actually happens next.

With chat AI — 8 steps, all yours

1. It prints code in the chat window.
2. You copy it.
3. You open a text editor.
4. You paste and save it with the right name, in the right folder.
5. You open it in a browser.
6. Something looks broken.
7. You copy the error back into the chat.
8. Repeat.

With a coding agent — 1 step

1. You say what you want.

It creates the file itself, in the right place, with the right name. It runs it. If something breaks, it reads its own error and fixes it — then tells you it's done.

You stayed in one place the whole time.
Walk down the left column out loud, slowly. The tedium is the point — everyone who has copy-pasted from ChatGPT feels this. The right column should feel like relief.
One thing to explain first

The terminal — talking to your computer with words

Coding agents live in a plain text window called the terminal. If you have never opened one, that is completely normal. It is only a different way of asking your computer to do things.

The way you know — pointing

You see folders and icons on screen. You move the mouse, double-click, and drag things around to make them happen.

You point at what you want.
The terminal — typing

The same computer, the same files. But instead of clicking, you type a line saying what you want, and press Enter.

You describe what you want.
Don't worry

You will not need to memorise commands. On Day 2 we practise the three or four you actually need — and after that, your agent does the typing for you.

Roughly a quarter of any room like this has never opened a terminal. Naming that openly - "that is completely normal" - keeps those people with you instead of quietly panicking for the next hour.

The pattern underneath

Everything an agent does is this one loop

What you just read follows the same four steps every single time — whether the task takes ten seconds or an hour.

Map each box back to the transcript they just saw. Then stress step 4: the human stays in charge. The agent never gets to decide it is finished — you do.
Its four powers

What the agent is actually allowed to do

A chat window can only produce text. An agent is that same AI, plus permission to touch your computer in four specific ways.

Read

Open and understand files already in your project, so its work fits with what's there.

Write

Create new files and edit existing ones, directly on your machine.

Run

Actually execute what it wrote — start the program, open the page, run the tests.

Observe

See the result or the error message, and use it to correct itself without being told.

The fourth one is what makes it feel like a colleague rather than a tool. It closes its own loop.

If someone asks "is it safe that it can write to my computer?" — good question, and the honest answer is it asks permission for anything significant, and you can always see what it changed. We cover this properly on Day 2.

Prompting

The agent is only as good as the instruction you give it. This is the most immediately useful half hour of the day — it improves your ChatGPT results tonight, too.
Instruction, not search Weak vs strong Anatomy of a prompt Six named techniques Which one, when
Sell this section: whatever else they forget, better prompting pays off immediately in work they already do. Flag that the six techniques coming up all have proper names they will see used online — knowing the names lets them read any AI article with confidence.
The mental shift

You are briefing an assistant, not searching Google

With a search engine, fewer words is better — you're matching keywords. With an AI, the opposite is true. More detail gives it more to work with.

Search habit

sports day page

Three words is enough, because you'll pick from a list of results yourself.

Briefing habit

"A page for our sports day on the 12th, with the event list, times, and house colours down the side."

There is no list to choose from — it builds exactly one thing, so it needs to know which.

Most people's poor AI results come from search-engine habits. Naming that habit explicitly is what breaks it.
Side by side

Weak

"Make a form"

What comes back: some form, with some fields, in some style. Probably not yours. You now spend ten minutes correcting it.

Vague in, vague out.
Strong

"Make a form with name, date and amount fields. Show an error if amount is blank. Keep the styling simple — no frameworks."

What comes back: close to right, first time.

Three extra sentences saved ten minutes.

Take it apart

What made that prompt work

a good prompt

Named techniques

Six ways to ask — and they all have proper names

These are the standard techniques people mean when they talk about "prompt engineering". You already use some of them by instinct — knowing the names means you can read any article about AI and follow it.

1. Zero-Shot

Just ask, with no examples. The everyday default.

2. Few-Shot

Show two or three examples so it copies your pattern.

3. Role / Persona

Tell it who to be, and who it is speaking to.

4. Chain-of-Thought

Ask it to reason step by step before answering.

5. Least-to-Most

Break a big job into small ones, in order.

6. Interview Style

Make it ask you questions first.

Reassure them this is not six things to memorise. They will use one or two constantly and the rest occasionally. The names matter mostly so nothing they read online sounds like jargon.
Technique 1 of 6

Zero-Shot — just ask, no examples

This is what everyone already does. It works well for simple, common tasks — and quietly disappoints when you had a particular format in mind but never said so.

Where it lets you down

"Write 5 quiz questions on photosynthesis."

You get five questions — but in whatever format and difficulty it chose. Probably not matching your paper.

You didn't say, so it guessed.
Where it shines

"Summarise this paragraph in one sentence."

Simple, unambiguous, only one sensible answer shape. No examples needed.

Perfect tool for a small job.
Important not to paint zero-shot as wrong — it is the right choice most of the time. The lesson is recognising the moment it stops being enough, which is exactly when the next five techniques start earning their keep.
Technique 2 of 6

Few-Shot — show it two or three examples

The fastest way to get output in your style is to show it your style, rather than trying to describe it in words.

Describing your style

"Write quiz questions in my usual style."

It has never seen your papers. It cannot know what your usual style is.

Asking for the impossible.
Showing your style

"Here are two of my questions:

1. State two uses of a spreadsheet. (2 marks)
2. Explain why a formula begins with =. (3 marks)

Now write three more in exactly this style."

It copies the pattern it can see.
This is the highest-value technique for teachers in the room — marks in brackets, question numbering, house phrasing all get picked up automatically. Suggest they keep two model questions in a file ready to paste.
Technique 3 of 6

Role / Persona — tell it who to be

Two sentences about who is speaking, and who is listening, changes the vocabulary, the depth and the examples it chooses.

No role given

"Explain photosynthesis."

You get a competent encyclopaedia paragraph, pitched at nobody in particular.

Correct, but unusable in class.
Role and audience given

"You are a Grade 8 science teacher in Sri Lanka. Explain photosynthesis to a 13-year-old using an example from a home garden. Under 150 words, no technical terms."

Now it is pitched at your class.
The audience half matters more than the role half. "Explain to a 13-year-old" does most of the work; "you are a teacher" mainly sets the tone. Worth saying so they do not over-invest in elaborate personas.
Technique 4 of 6

Chain-of-Thought — make it show its working

Remember it predicts one token at a time. If you let it jump straight to an answer, it commits before it has reasoned. Asking for the steps first makes it reason on the way.

Straight to the verdict

"A student answered 47 for this problem. Is that correct?"

You get a confident yes or no — sometimes the wrong one.

It guessed before it thought.
Working first, verdict after

"Solve this problem yourself, step by step. Then compare your answer with the student's 47, and tell me exactly where they went wrong."

Better answer — and a markable explanation.
Tie this straight back to the tokens slide: the model literally thinks by writing, so giving it room to write is giving it room to think. This is the technique for anything involving numbers, logic or marking.
Technique 5 of 6

Least-to-Most — build up in small steps

For anything large, one giant request produces something shallow. Asking in sequence, where each answer feeds the next, produces something you can actually use.

One giant ask

"Create a full term scheme of work for Grade 10 ICT."

Generic, thin, and wrong in ways that are tedious to correct.

Too big to be good.
Three asks, in order

1. "List the topics for a 10-week Grade 10 ICT term."

2. "For topic 1, list the learning outcomes."

3. "Now write week 1's lesson plan using those outcomes."

Each step checked before the next.
This is the seed of the entire Day 2 and Day 4 workflow — idea, then spec, then plan, then tasks. Say that explicitly: they are meeting the method now in miniature and will run it properly on Thursday.
Technique 6 of 6

Interview Style — make it ask you first

The most counter-intuitive one, and often the best. Instead of trying to describe everything up front, hand the questioning over and let it find the gaps.

You guess what matters

"Make me a revision worksheet on spreadsheets."

It fills every gap with an assumption — year group, length, difficulty, format.

You correct it afterwards.
It finds the gaps for you

"Before you write anything, ask me five questions that would help you make this worksheet well. Then wait for my answers."

The gaps get found before the work.
This is exactly what the idea-refine skill does on Day 2 and Day 4 — so when it starts interrogating them on Thursday, they will recognise the behaviour instead of finding it odd. Worth naming that link now.

Habits that pay off

Four things worth doing every time

  • Say what you actually want — name the fields, the pages, the wording. The machine cannot see what's in your head.
  • Give the background — what already exists, who it's for, where it fits. Context prevents wrong assumptions.
  • Set boundaries — "no frameworks", "don't change the database", "keep it under one page".
  • Ask for the plan before the work — for anything bigger than a small change, "tell me your plan first" catches misunderstandings while they're still cheap.
The fourth habit is the seed of the whole Day 4 workflow — plan first, then build. Flag it as something they will do properly later in the week.
The right expectation

Treat the first answer as a first draft

Even an excellent prompt often lands at eighty percent. That is not failure — that is the normal working rhythm, and it is much faster than starting from a blank page.

What beginners do

The result isn't right, so they delete everything and rewrite the whole request from scratch.

Slow. Loses the good parts.
What to do instead

"Close. The date field should be first, and the error message should be red."

Keeps the 80%. Fixes the 20%.
This is the standing rule for every hands-on session this week: don't start over, say what's wrong. Repeat it on Days 2, 3 and 4 until it is automatic.

Term 3 of 5 · The problem

Sometimes you don't know the right steps to ask for

A command saves an instruction you already know how to write. But what if you don't know the professional way to do something in the first place?

Without a skill

"Help me plan an app."

You get something generic, because neither of you has agreed on what "planning an app" involves.

With a skill

The agent already has an expert's step-by-step method for that job written down, and follows it: ask questions, explore options, narrow down, write a spec.

You borrow someone else's expertise.
Frame a skill as borrowed expertise. The person who wrote it knew a good process; you get the benefit without knowing it yourself.

One agent doing everything holds too much in its head at once. Instead it hands focused pieces to helpers — and OpenCode already ships with several, ready to use.

scout

MCP — one standard way to plug in outside tools

Your agent knows about your files. It does not automatically know about your school database, your calendar, or anything else outside the folder. MCP is the agreed standard for connecting those in — so every tool doesn't need its own special wiring.

Think of it as

A universal travel adapter. Rather than a different plug for every country, one agreed shape fits them all. You won't need this in our four days — but you'll hear the word constantly, so now you know.

Be honest that this is the one term they will not touch this week. Knowing what it means when they read about it is enough.

Being straight with you

Where it will let you down

You will meet all three of these this week. None of them are your fault, and none of them mean you did something wrong.

Confident mistakes

Remember it predicts likely text. Sometimes the likely-sounding thing is wrong — stated with total confidence. Always check the result yourself.

It pauses

Free accounts have limits. Occasionally it stops for a minute mid-task. That is normal, not broken — wait, then carry on.

Different every time

Ask twice, get two slightly different answers. Neither is a bug. It's why your instructions matter more than luck.

Saying this openly on Day 1 pays off all week: when it happens on Day 4, nobody panics or assumes they broke something. The honesty also builds trust in everything else you have claimed.
